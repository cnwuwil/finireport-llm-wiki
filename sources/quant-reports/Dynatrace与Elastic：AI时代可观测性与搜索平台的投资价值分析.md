# Theme #1: From Visibility to Operational Control We believe observability is moving up the stack from visibility toward execution.

The category was originally built to make increasingly complex software environments easier to see and diagnose. That remains foundational, but we believe the center of gravity is shifting. As architectures have become more distributed and release cycles more continuous, vendors are increasingly orienting product roadmaps around faster diagnosis, remediation, and operational response rather than pure telemetry visualization. In our view, the value proposition is moving closer to shortening the path from signal to action – and, over time, automating more of that loop altogether.

The shift is increasingly visible in how the market is being built. Dynatrace’s launch of Dynatrace Intelligence (Jan. 2026) framed the platform around agentic operations and trusted automation, while Datadog’s Workflow Automation and Bits AI SRE point in a similar direction by unifying observability data to support investigation and response. Elastic’s Observability AI assistant is a more incremental example, but the direction of travel is clear. We believe observability is gradually evolving from a system of insight into more of an operating layer for modern software environments, and the vendors best positioned will be those that can pair broad telemetry coverage with strong context, automation, and workflow depth – not simply collect more data but help customers operationalize it.

As observability transitions from a system of record to a system of action, the surface area for value capture expands materially. By integrating earlier into the software development lifecycle - shaping testing and application design - and becoming more central to live operations, platforms are evolving from visibility tools into a mission-critical operating layer. This broader footprint provides a more robust framework for underwriting ROI. In our discussions with private companies, value is increasingly framed through two primary levers: the quantifiable cost of downtime and the compression of SRE labor. In this model, human effort becomes a measurable cost pool that software can actively reduce. We believe the category is heading toward a participation model: as automation becomes more tangible, the best-positioned platforms will be those that reliably translate telemetry into trusted action, allowing them to capture a portion of the significant productivity gains they create.

Theme #3: OpenTelemetry Raises the Competitive Bar OpenTelemetry is reshaping observability by decoupling telemetry collection from proprietary agents that historically defined the category. For much of observability’s history, platforms differentiated through tightly integrated agents that controlled how telemetry was collected, enriched, and correlated, which created strong lock‑in but limited flexibility. OpenTelemetry changes that dynamic by standardizing how logs, metrics, and traces are generated and routed, giving customers more freedom in how telemetry is instrumented and where it is sent, and reducing proprietary collection as a standalone point of control. That said, open collection does not eliminate the value of approaches that simplify deployment, enrich telemetry with system‑level context, and deliver a more consistent and reliable view of production behavior, which helps explain why Dynatrace’s OneAgent remains relevant. Both Dynatrace and Elastic have been aligned with this shift for some time: Dynatrace supports OpenTelemetry ingestion alongside OneAgent‑based enrichment, while Elastic has long supported native OTLP 订阅研报添加微信 LCD123321Cad 04-21 workflows and introduced its Distributions of OpenTelemetry (EDOT) in 2024. In our view, the key takeaway is that OpenTelemetry is no longer an alternative architecture, but the market baseline, with differentiation increasingly accruing above the collection layer rather than at it.

The more critical implication is that competitive advantage is migrating to the highest levels of the stack. This shift is not a new phenomenon, but it has become the central industry tension: as telemetry becomes portable, the focal point of competition moves past the collection layer and even the processing plane toward the decision and action tier. While proprietary agents were never the primary source of value, the transition toward Open Standards as the market baseline has increased the onus on vendors to distinguish themselves through the intelligence that drives automated remediation and business outcomes. In our view, the processing plane, where data is shaped, routed, and governed, is becoming a strategic prerequisite, but the true competitive moat lies in the platform’s ability to translate that data into decisive actions. Dynatrace’s April 2026 acquisition of Bindplane is a clear signal of this transition, reflecting the need to control telemetry upstream so that the downstream decision engine can operate on high-fidelity, cost-optimized context. The vendors best positioned for this era are those that treat open collection as a commodity baseline and focus their innovation on the analytical intelligence and automated workflows that transform raw signals into proactive operational decisions.

Prometheus was an early signal that observability was already moving towards more open and modular architectures. It remains the foundational open-source standard for cloud-native metrics, and vendors such as Grafana, Chronosphere and Datadog all support Prometheus-compatible workflows in some form. OpenTelemetry 订阅研报添加微信 LCD123321Cad 04-21 pushes that same direction further by extending portability beyond metrics and across multiple signals. In our view, both technologies are best understood as part of the same broader architectural trend: more open collection, more modular telemetry stacks and a higher burden on platforms to differentiate above the data layer.

# Theme #4: AI Expands Both Markets

We believe AI is making modern software environments harder to monitor and more important to understand. On the observability side, AI workloads introduce new sources of complexity around latency, token consumption, reliability, model behavior, agent reliability, and cost control. That expands both the amount of telemetry enterprises need and the importance of interpreting that telemetry correctly across models, orchestration layers, tools and downstream infrastructure. Dynatrace has leaned directly into that trend with its AI and LLM observability offering, which is designed to provide real-time visibility into LLMs, AI agents, orchestration layers and their downstream application and infrastructure impact, and more recently broadened support for additional agentic AI frameworks and protocols. In our view, AI is not simply adding incremental workload volume; it is introducing a new class of production systems with non-deterministic behavior, opaque failure modes, and cost profiles that traditional application monitoring was not designed to manage.

At the same time, AI is raising the strategic value of retrieval infrastructure. RAG and agentic workflows depend on fast, relevant access to enterprise data, which increases the importance of hybrid search, vector search, filtering and reranking quality. We believe this should be a tailwind for Elastic at the back-end retrieval layer, where its search heritage and hybrid capabilities are directly relevant. At the same time, this remains a highly competitive part of the stack, with alternatives spanning open-source 订阅研报添加微信 LCD123321Cad 04-21 (Postgres), MongoDB, Hyperscalers and specialist providers. Elastic is trying to differentiate with Agent Builder and tighter Jina AI-powered embeddings and reranking inside Elastic Inference Service – but we think the clearer benefit today is at the retrieval layer, while the eventual winners higher up the agent stack remain less settled.

# Theme #5: Data Economics Shape the Next Leg of Growth We believe data economics have become one of the category’s defining fault lines.

The historical friction started in the Splunk era, where ingest-and-index economics often forced customers to ration visibility because retaining and querying data volumes

became expensive quickly. The shift to cloud-native platforms improved usability and broadened workflow value, but it did not eliminate the underlying tension once log volumes scaled. In our view, data economics are no longer a secondary consideration but a gating factor in platform selection, as customers increasingly evaluate whether they can afford to retain, query, and operationalize more data over time.

Observability is entering a phase where architecture matters at least as much as product breadth. Some companies are attacking the problem at the storage and query layer through lower-cost log architectures, lighter indexing, or more modular back ends. Other are attacking it even earlier, at the point of collection, by helping customers filter, 订阅研报添加微信 LCD123321Cad 04-21 enrich, and route telemetry before it ever lands in a premium destination. In our view, that first-mile shift is important because it changes where observability economics are determined. Once noisy or low-value data reaches an expensive back end, much of the cost has already compounded. Companies like Cribl are pressing that point directly, and the broader implication is that destination platforms are facing pressure not just from cheaper storage, but from upstream control over where data gets sent, retained and queried in the first place.

Against that backdrop, we think the market is likely to support both modular architectures and broader premium platforms. The modular path should appeal to customers optimizing for lower-cost back ends, more composable tooling, and tighter control over telemetry spend. The broader platform path should appeal to customers willing to pay more for unified data, tighter workflow integration, reliability, and a more complete operating layer. In our view, Dynatrace fits firmly in the latter camp, while Elastic straddles a middle-ground between a high-performance data backend and an increasingly capable operating layer. We do not believe being the lowest-cost endpoint is a prerequisite for success. But it does require both companies to deliver enough value through unification, context and workflow depth to justify a premium as data volumes continue to grow.

Theme #6: Buyers Want Fewer Platforms We believe observability is entering a more explicit consolidation cycle. Tool sprawl was tolerable when the category was younger and buyer were optimizing for

best-of-breed functionality at the point-solution level. That is less true today. As observability estates have become larger and more expensive, the cost of fragmentation becomes harder to ignore – not just in vendor spend, but in workflow friction, duplicated data and operational complexity. We believe this is central to why the market is beginning to place greater value on platforms that can unify more of the workflow on shared data and shared context, with clearer governance and cost controls over what gets collected, stored, and acted upon.

Market signals underscore this is becoming a real source of demand. Grafana’s Observability survey (2025) showed $64 \%$ of respondents now have less than five observability technologies (up from $4 4 \%$ last year) and ETR’s 2025 survey indicates more than half of respondents are planning to consolidate further over the next two years. The market is increasingly responding to that same trend: Cisco’s acquisition of Splunk pulled a scaled observability asset into a broader networking, security, and operations platform; Palo Alto’s acquisition of Chronosphere reflects the growing value of telemetry inside security control planes; Snowflake’s acquisition of Observe points to observability becoming more relevant inside data and AI stacks. In our view, consolidation is changing the basis of competition from feature leadership in isolated tools to ownership of shared telemetry, context, and response workflows across the estate. Vendors increasingly need to prove they can consolidate spend, displace adjacent tools, and own a broader share of the telemetry estate.

# Valuation and Key Risks
